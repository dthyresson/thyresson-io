_This post is the written version of my breakout session, **Smells Like Team Spirit: Startups as a Band**, at the [Northeast Entrepreneurship Summit](https://luma.com/au81vqz3?tk=rVCbrU), hosted by The Eleven Society, on Sunday, November 15._

_**Session abstract:** Startups are usually called a team sport. They're closer to a band: a small group of people making something together under pressure, on the road, with too little sleep and too much at stake. This session is about how to pick your team: the people you'll work with for the next five or ten years, why solo founders play every instrument, and how to argue without breaking up the band._

_I'm also judging at the summit. The session is intentionally small and informal: a conversation for liberal arts undergrads from NESCAC schools, not a lecture. More on [The Eleven Society](https://www.theelevensociety.com/)._

## Startup as a Band

You have ⚪ solo startups where one person tries to fill all roles and play all instruments.

You have ▬ duos where one person does the business and the other the technology.

You have core founder ▲ groups where each has its function, but together they deliver the vision.

A 🔲 **quartet** gives you more capacity to scale. You can balance more responsibilities, ideate more products or features simultaneously, or engage with customers and investors in more ways so strategic growth becomes more feasible.

## Who Starts Up Startups?

Can you start a company alone? Yes. Plenty of people do. But you're playing every instrument. You're the songwriter, the salesperson, the therapist, the CFO. There's no one to catch the mistake, no one to tell you the truth, no one to share the load.

The risk isn't just burnout. It's blind spots. A solo founder can move fast and still build the wrong thing very efficiently.

Every band needs a dreamer, a technologist, and an entrepreneur. The trick is getting them in the same room.

Some people thrive solo. If that's you, build a band around you: advisors, early employees, a peer group. You don't have to be a duo to have a band. But you do need people who will tell you when the song is bad.

**Startups are hard. You can't do everything alone. Finding the right team and having clear roles and responsibilities is key to success.**

## What Kind of Band Is Your Startup?

You're not picking a co-founder for the launch. You're picking the person you'll be in a van with for the next five or ten years.

That's the test. Not "Can we build this?" but "Do I want to be on the road with this person when it gets hard?"

Look for complementary skills, shared values, clear roles, and trust. Not identical tastes. Shared standards. You want someone who will argue with you about the work and still have your back.

Ask yourself:

- Would I want this person in the room when something goes wrong?
- Can we divide the work without keeping score?
- Do we agree on what we're optimizing for?
- Can we disagree without making it personal?

Roles matter. In a band, someone plays drums, someone plays bass, someone writes the songs. In a startup, someone owns the product, someone owns the customer, someone owns the money. Ambiguity is where resentment grows.

## Bands Thrive on Energy, Grow with Structure, and Compound Success

  height={405}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

Startups are a roadshow. Fundraising is a roadshow. Sales is a roadshow. You travel, pitch, repeat yourself, sleep badly, eat badly, and miss the people you love. The pressure doesn't stop when you raise the round. It changes shape.

**Startups aren't just ideas – they are businesses. And businesses become complex… fast. Find your ICP. Find PMF. Find great design partners and people to help you on the way.**

That complexity and overhead also present themselves when choosing the technology, architecture, and services needed to deliver on a startup's vision. What do I use? Who do I trust? Can I afford them? Am I betting on the right partner? That slows a startup down.

It's the team that's gonna get you through that. Not a better stack or a bigger deck. The people who can pick up the parts you can't carry.

The pressure is insane. The stress is real. On the road, small things become big things. Someone is late. Someone isn't carrying the amp. Someone gets the credit. Someone gets the blame. The band starts to fray.

Conflict is normal. Arguments are going to happen. The question is not whether you'll fight. It's whether you can fight well.

Bands break up over money, ego, direction, and exhaustion. Startups do too.

Fight about the work, not the person. Fight in private. Say the thing directly. Assume good intent. Keep decision rights clear. Apologize when you're wrong. Don't keep score. Revisit the setlist.

**A little chaos can channel creative energy into great things, including many of the best innovations.** The trick is keeping the chaos from becoming the business.

Sometimes the right move is to change the song, not the band.

Some breakups are necessary. If values are misaligned or trust is gone, leaving is better than staying. But don't confuse a bad gig with a bad band.

## Talent & Creativity Become a Business

The skills that keep a band together are liberal arts skills: empathy, communication, reading people, understanding context, making an argument, changing your mind. You don't need to be the best musician in the room. You need to know how to play with other people.

**Every startup needs that creative spark and the talent to deliver.**

Startups are hard. The road is long. The pressure is real. You will have arguments. You may have a bad gig.

The goal is not to avoid any of that. The goal is to choose the people you want to go through it with.

Pick people you'd want to be on the road with for the next five or ten years. Then don't break up over a bad gig.

The song changes. The band is what gets you to the next one.

You don't have to do it alone. And if you find the right four, you can find your Ringo.
