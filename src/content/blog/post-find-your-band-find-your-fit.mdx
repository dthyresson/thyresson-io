The KLF were two men following their own recipe of "little money or talent": an ex-label A&R man and a squat-dwelling guitarist.

They were not virtuosos.

They sampled and collided other people's records, put five singles in the UK top ten, and in 1991 were the biggest-selling singles act in the world.

Then they quit the music business. Two years later they took a million pounds in cash to a Scottish island and burned it. More on that at the end.

Silicon Valley has spent decades trying to write the same book under a different title: product-market fit.

Does the product work? Does anyone want it? Do they want it enough to use it, pay for it, and keep coming back?

The usual story is about a founder with a vision. The truer story is about a band.

Cursor spent years on the wrong product before the same team found the right one.

fal built Python data infrastructure before the same team pointed it at generative media.

Workbrew found a business in the gap between the developers who use Homebrew and the IT departments that pay to manage it.

We treat product-market fit as a product milestone, something a product achieves. It is really a team behavior.

The team questions the assumption. The team notices the new market. The team holds the point of view. The team understands the buyer. The team connects the lifecycle.

## Startup as a Band

<Image
  src={quartetImg}
  alt="The quartet: four founders with more capacity to scale"
  width={720}
  height={405}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

- **Solo:** one person fills every role.
- **Duos:** one person does the business, the other the technology.
- **Trios:** each founder has a function, but together they deliver the vision.
- **Quartet:** more capacity to scale, more responsibilities, more product bets in parallel, more ways to engage customers and investors.

There is no perfect lineup. There is only the lineup you choose and the people you choose to be in it with.

You're not picking a co-founder for the launch. You're picking the person you'll be in a van with for the next five or ten years.

That's the test. Not "Can we build this?" but "Do I want to be on the road with this person when it gets hard?"

Look for complementary skills, shared values, clear roles, and trust. Not identical tastes, but shared standards.

You want someone who will argue with you about the work and still have your back.

<blockquote className="callout">
  <p>
    <strong>
      Would I want this person in the room when something goes wrong? Can we
      divide the work without keeping score? Do we agree on what we're
      optimizing for? Can we disagree without making it personal?
    </strong>
  </p>
</blockquote>

### Who's in the van?

Can you start a company alone? Yes. Plenty of people do.

But you're playing every instrument: songwriter, salesperson, therapist, CFO.

There's no one to catch the mistake, no one to tell you the truth, no one to share the load.

The risk isn't only burnout; it's blind spots. A solo founder can move fast and build the wrong thing very efficiently.

Every band needs a dreamer, a technologist, and an entrepreneur, and the trick is getting them in the same room.

If you thrive solo, build a band around you: advisors, early employees, a peer group.

You don't have to be a duo to have a band, but you do need people who will tell you when the song is bad.

## The Band Finds the Fit

The PMF stories are usually told as product stories. Told again, they are team stories.

In each company below, the thing that changed was not the founders' talent or the size of the market. It was what the team was willing to notice, question, and do.

### Doctorin' the Product / Last Train to Model Central

<Image
  src={cursorArt}
  alt="Cursor, Doctorin' the Product, after The Timelords' Doctorin' the Tardis"
  width={448}
  height={448}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

#### <img src="/logos/cursor.png" alt="Cursor logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Cursor: change the product

Anysphere started in 2022 with an AI email client and an AI-powered CAD tool for mechanical engineers. The CAD project was also called Cursor at first.

They pivoted to code. Even after finding the direction, they kept improving the product.

PWV (Preston-Werner Ventures) first saw them at the seed round, when the product and developer experience still needed work.

They saw them again at the Series A, when the same founders and the same thesis had a dramatically better product.

The first idea was a hypothesis, not a destiny. The team's willingness to kill it is what found the fit.

_What's the hypothesis you're treating as destiny, and what would it take to test it this month?_

<Image
  src={falArt}
  alt="fal, Last Train to Model Central, after The KLF's Last Train to Trancentral"
  width={448}

  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

#### <img src="/logos/fal.png" alt="fal logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> fal: change the market

Burkay and Gorkem started by building Python data infrastructure for data pipelines, analytics, and ML training.

It was highly optimized, but it was not the business that made them successful.

PWV had been looking for a platform to generate images, video, and music, and most demos were slow or clumsy.

Then fal's "Make it Real" collaboration with tldraw made an image appear milliseconds after you sketched on screen.

Their infrastructure was exceptionally good at running generative models at scale.

When Stable Diffusion took off, they pointed it at the new market.

The team recognized an opportunity that was already adjacent to the work.

_What infrastructure or skill you've already built could you point at a completely different market?_

### What Time Is Fit?

<Image
  src={tursoArt}
  alt="Turso, What Time Is Fit?, after The KLF's What Time Is Love? (Pure Trance 1)"
  width={448}
  height={448}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

#### <img src="/logos/turso.png" alt="Turso logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Turso: the market catches up

Turso built for unlimited SQLite databases: one per user, one per agent, one per task.

Its multi-tenant isolation and local-first experience were already suited for the agentic era.

PWV invested in the angel and seed rounds.

Glauber believed the future was millions of small databases, which at the time was an emerging idea, not a market.

Then AI agents arrived. They need to spin up a database as easily as creating a file, with as little concern about cost.

Turso had the right shape because the team had a point of view before the market did.

In October 2026, the company that won the previous era acquired the company built for the next one.

_What's a bet you're making on where the market will be in two years, and how will you know if you're early or just wrong?_

<Image
  src={workbrewArt}
  alt="Workbrew, 3 A.M. Eternal, after The KLF's 3 A.M. Eternal"
  width={448}
  height={448}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

#### <img src="/logos/workbrew.png" alt="Workbrew logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Workbrew: find the buyer

Developers love Homebrew, the free tool for installing software on macOS and Linux.

Corporate IT departments do not love employees installing whatever they want; they need visibility, security, and control.

Workbrew built a business in that gap. Developers use the software; companies pay to manage it.

Two groups can look at exactly the same product and see completely different problems, and the team that understands both is the one that finds the business.

_What's a free tool developers use that companies would pay to bring under control, and what would each side say they're buying?_

### Justified & Validated?

<Image
  src={zooArt}
  alt="Zoo, Justified & Validated, after The KLF's Justified & Ancient"
  width={448}
  height={448}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

#### <img src="/logos/zoo.png" alt="Zoo logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Zoo: become the layer

CAD has been one of the most entrenched software markets in the world for over 50 years: proprietary file formats, workstation-bound execution, and workflows never designed for cloud compute or machine learning.

Hardware design data mostly sits in static files that are hard to use as training data.

Zoo rebuilt the CAD stack from first principles: KCL turns design intent into machine-readable code, and a GPU-backed geometry engine runs through an API.

It does not have to replace the incumbents. It plugs into existing stacks, connecting design, analysis, and manufacturing.

Blue Origin is already feeding millions of CAD files into a fine-tuned Text-to-CAD model, early proof that Zoo can expand the market rather than only compete inside it.

The team connected the lifecycle instead of defending a single tool.

_What's one deeply entrenched market you would never try to replace, and what layer could you build underneath it?_

## Bands Thrive on Energy, Grow with Structure, and Compound Success

<Image
  src={thriveImg}
  alt="Bands thrive on energy, grow with structure, and channel chaos into compound success"
  width={720}
  height={405}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

Finding fit changes the job.

After the raise there is a board, a hiring plan, and payroll. After PMF the customers arrive at once and want uptime, support, security reviews, and the roadmap.

The club tour becomes an arena tour. The songs you wrote for forty people have to fill a stadium.

This is when teams break up. Not on the way up but at the top.

So here are the lessons for the next stage:

- **Renegotiate roles.** The job that found fit is not the job that scales it.
- **Keep decision rights clear.** Ambiguity is where resentment grows.
- **Hire for the next stage.** The person who got you here may not be the person who gets you there.
- **Add structure without killing the energy.** Process should make the work easier, not slower.
- **Keep the feedback loop short.** Fight about the work, not the person.
- **Expect the pressure to change shape.** The raise and PMF both add weight.
- **Keep the setlist fresh.** What worked for forty people may not fill a stadium.
- **Don't break up over one bad gig.** A bad quarter is not a failed company.

The product may be the reason customers show up. The team is the reason the company is still there when they do.

## WANTED: Drummer. Tech Co-Founder. Dreamer.

<Image
  src={perfectImg}
  alt="The Perfect Co-Founder: a vintage Roxy Music-style classified ad"
  width={720}
  height={538}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

Startups are hard, the road is long, and the pressure is real, so you will have arguments and you may have a bad gig.

The goal is not to avoid any of that. The goal is to choose the people you want to go through it with.

<blockquote className="callout">
  <p>
    <strong>
      Pick people you'd want to be on the road with for the next five or ten
      years. Then don't break up over one poor performance.
    </strong>
  </p>
</blockquote>

The lineup you launch with is not always the lineup you win with, and the van will tell you.

You don't have to do it alone. And if you find the right four, you can find your Ringo.

The KLF wrote the manual, found the audience, and then quit. Two years later they burned a million pounds on Jura.

Fit is not a finish line: you find it, lose it, and re-earn it.

The world decides whether it wants what you built. You decide who is in the van with you when you find out.
