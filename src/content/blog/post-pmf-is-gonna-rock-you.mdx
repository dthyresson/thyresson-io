_This post is the written version of my breakout session, **PMF Is Gonna Rock Ya!**, at [The Eleven Society Summit](https://luma.com/au81vqz3?tk=rVCbrU) on Sunday, November 15._

_**Session abstract:** A breakout session for The Eleven Society Summit: five lessons on product-market fit from Cursor, fal, Turso, Workbrew, and Zoo, with a detour through The KLF, The Manual, and a million pounds burned on Jura._

_I'm also judging at the summit. The session is intentionally small and informal — a conversation for liberal arts undergrads from NESCAC schools, not a lecture. More on [The Eleven Society](https://www.theelevensociety.com/)._

In the late 1980s, Bill Drummond and Jimmy Cauty made records as The JAMs, The Timelords, and eventually The KLF. They weren't virtuoso musicians; they sampled, borrowed, and combined things in unexpected ways, and became one of the most successful pop acts of their time.

Then they wrote [_The Manual: How to Have a Number One the Easy Way_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Manual) (1988): a tongue-in-cheek guide to a No. 1 with no money or musical skills, using their novelty hit "Doctorin' the Tardis" as the case study.

The advice was blunt: be skint and on the dole, because "having no money sharpens the wits"; if you're already a musician, "stop playing your instrument. Even better, sell the junk." The book also predicted the home-recording revolution — soon you could "do the whole thing at home" and "sod off all that crap about going into studios."

Drummond's point was simpler than a business plan: "If you want to do something, go and do it! Don't wait to be asked."

Silicon Valley has been trying to write the same book for decades. We call it **product-market fit. PMF.** Does the product work, does anyone want it, and do they want it enough to use it, pay for it, and keep coming back?

You don't need to invent a new technology. Sometimes the opportunity is recognizing what existing technology makes possible.

Here are five examples from companies we've invested in at [Preston-Werner Ventures](https://www.pwv.com).

## Doctorin' the Product / Last Train to Model Central

<Image
  src={cursorArt}
  alt="Cursor — Doctorin' the Product, after The Timelords' Doctorin' the Tardis"
  width={448}
  height={448}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

### <img src="/logos/cursor.png" alt="Cursor logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Cursor — change the product

We first looked at Cursor at its seed round.

Frankly, the product wasn't great.

We liked the founders. We believed AI would fundamentally change how software gets written. But the product wasn't there yet.

Four months later, at the Series A, we saw it again.

It was dramatically better.

Same founders. Same opportunity. Very different product.

Anysphere had started in 2022, exploring different ideas before focusing on Cursor. They'd already spent considerable time figuring out what to build.

And even after finding the right direction, they kept improving it.

**The lesson: your first idea is a hypothesis, not your destiny.**

Good founders challenge their own assumptions. They look at the evidence and change their minds.

That's something you practice in a liberal arts education. It's also essential to building a company.

<Image
  src={falArt}
  alt="fal — Last Train to Model Central, after The KLF's Last Train to Trancentral"
  width={448}
  height={448}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

### <img src="/logos/fal.png" alt="fal logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> fal — change the market

fal's story is almost the opposite.

They started by building Python data science infrastructure.

But they discovered that what they'd built was exceptionally good at running AI models that generate images and video.

Then generative media took off.

They recognized that their technology had a much more valuable application than the one they'd started with.

So they changed the market they served.

**Cursor changed the product. fal changed the market.**

Neither started with exactly the business that made it successful.

And neither had to invent all the underlying technology.

They recognized an opportunity, made something useful out of it, and followed the evidence.

**Sometimes the best opportunity isn't the one you set out to find.**

## What Time Is Fit?

<Image
  src={tursoArt}
  alt="Turso — What Time Is Fit?, after The KLF's What Time Is Love? (Pure Trance 1)"
  width={448}
  height={448}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

### <img src="/logos/turso.png" alt="Turso logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Turso — the market catches up

I consulted with Supabase back in 2021.

Supabase took databases, something complicated and fairly intimidating, and made them much easier for developers to use.

So when we invested in Turso, I asked its founder, Glauber Costa, a fairly obvious question:

**"Why aren't you building Supabase for SQLite?"**

SQLite is an incredibly popular lightweight database. Why not take the same approach?

Glauber had a different point of view.

He believed the future was millions of small, independent databases.

One for every user. Every AI agent. Every task.

At the time, that was an interesting architectural idea. But was there really a market for it?

Then AI agents started becoming a reality.

Suddenly, having millions of isolated databases looked much more valuable.

And in October 2026, Supabase acquired Turso.

Apparently, they saw something in that future too.

**Sometimes you don't find the market. The market finds you.**

Glauber's advantage wasn't just technical. He had a different perspective on how software would work in the future.

<Image

  alt="Workbrew — 3 A.M. Eternal, after The KLF's 3 A.M. Eternal"

### <img src="/logos/workbrew.png" alt="Workbrew logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Workbrew — find the buyer

Workbrew solves a different problem.

Developers love Homebrew. It's a free tool for installing software on Macs.

But corporate IT departments don't necessarily love employees installing whatever they want.

They need visibility, security, and control.

Workbrew recognized the opportunity between those two groups.

Developers use the software. Companies pay to manage it.

We invested because we saw an opportunity to build a business around something developers already loved.

**The person who uses your product isn't always the person who pays for it.**

This is really about understanding people.

Two groups can look at exactly the same product and see completely different problems.

Recognizing those differences is often where the business opportunity lies.

## Justified & Validated?

<Image
  src={zooArt}
  alt="Zoo — Justified & Validated, after The KLF's Justified & Ancient"
  width={448}
  height={448}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

### <img src="/logos/zoo.png" alt="Zoo logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Zoo — interest is not adoption

Zoo is another of our investments, and this story is still unfolding.

Think about how engineers design physical things.

Aircraft parts. Cars. Rocket engines.

They use sophisticated computer-aided design software, but much of the process is still manual.

Zoo is building technology that lets engineers use software and AI to generate designs.

It's ambitious. And the technology is impressive.

But the question for us isn't just whether it works.

**Will engineers actually change how they work?**

Zoo has promising pilots and early customers. But a demonstration isn't the same as a product people depend on.

People have habits. Organizations have established workflows. Engineers have years of experience with existing tools.

Even if something is technically better, people don't automatically adopt it.

So what do we look for?

Do customers come back?

Does a pilot become a production deployment?

Do people depend on the product?

Will they pay for it year after year?

**If everyone loves your demo but nobody changes their behavior, do you have product-market fit?**

Probably not.

This is why understanding people matters.

Technology can make something possible. But people decide whether it becomes valuable.

**A great product solves a technical problem. A successful product also solves a human problem.**

Five companies. Five different lessons.

| ------------ | -------------------- | ----------------------- |
| <img src="/logos/cursor.png" alt="Cursor logo" width="20" height="20" class="inline-block h-5 w-5 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> **Cursor**   | Change the product   | Question assumptions    |
| <img src="/logos/fal.png" alt="fal logo" width="20" height="20" class="inline-block h-5 w-5 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> **fal**      | Change the market    | Recognize opportunities |
| <img src="/logos/turso.png" alt="Turso logo" width="20" height="20" class="inline-block h-5 w-5 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> **Turso**    | Get the timing right | Have a point of view    |
| <img src="/logos/workbrew.png" alt="Workbrew logo" width="20" height="20" class="inline-block h-5 w-5 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> **Workbrew** | Find the buyer       | Understand people       |
| <img src="/logos/zoo.png" alt="Zoo logo" width="20" height="20" class="inline-block h-5 w-5 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> **Zoo**      | Prove adoption       | Understand behavior     |

There's no single formula for product-market fit. Much of what makes great founders successful has little to do with being the best programmer in the room: curiosity, creativity, taste, critical thinking, understanding people, and the willingness to change your mind. Those aren't exclusive to liberal arts graduates, but they're exactly what a liberal arts education develops.

You don't need MIT or Stanford to recognize a problem worth solving, and you don't have to invent the underlying technology. You can learn the tools, find collaborators, or bring together complementary skills. What matters is having a point of view about how the world could be different — and being willing to find out whether you're right.

Which brings me back to The KLF. They didn't invent the instruments. They took what already existed, combined it in original ways, and made a mark on popular culture. They found an audience, became enormously successful, and even wrote the manual.

Then, in 1992, they quit the music business. Two years later they took a million pounds in cash to a Scottish island called Jura and burned it. Literally.

As a venture capitalist, I've seen plenty of startups burn through a million pounds — but rarely quite so efficiently. It was a deliberate artistic act, not a business failure. And it makes one last point: **finding product-market fit and building a lasting business are two different things.** You can find fit, lose it, or walk away from it.

The KLF had the creativity, taste, and point of view to make something people wanted. That's the connection I want to leave you with: **you don't have to invent the instruments to change the music.** You need an idea, the imagination to do something with it, and enough curiosity to discover whether anyone else cares.

Because ultimately, **you don't get to decide whether the world wants what you've built. The world decides.**

### PMF Is Gonna Rock Ya!
