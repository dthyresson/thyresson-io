_This post is the written version of my breakout session, **PMF Is Gonna Rock Ya!**, at the [Northeast Entrepreneurship Summit](https://luma.com/au81vqz3?tk=rVCbrU), hosted by The Eleven Society, on Sunday, November 15._

_I'm also judging at the summit. The session is intentionally small and informal: a conversation for liberal arts undergrads from NESCAC schools, not a lecture. More on [The Eleven Society](https://www.theelevensociety.com/)._

In the late 1980s and early 1990s, Bill Drummond and Jimmy Cauty recorded under a series of overlapping names: the Justified Ancients of Mu Mu (the JAMs), the Timelords, and the KLF. Neither was a virtuoso; they were conceptualists who sampled, borrowed, and collided other people's records, often without permission. In 1991, as the KLF, they were the biggest-selling singles act in the world.

Then they wrote [_The Manual: How to Have a Number One the Easy Way_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Manual) (first published in 1988): a tongue-in-cheek guide to a No. 1 with no money or musical skills, using their novelty hit "Doctorin' the Tardis" as the case study.

The advice was blunt: be skint and on the dole, because "having no money sharpens the wits"; if you're already a musician, "stop playing your instrument. Even better, sell the junk." The book also predicted the home-recording revolution: soon you could "do the whole thing at home" and "sod off all that crap about going into studios."

Drummond's point was simpler than a business plan: "If you want to do something, go and do it! Don't wait to be asked..."

Silicon Valley has been trying to write the same book for decades. We call it **product-market fit. PMF.** Does the product work, does anyone want it, and do they want it enough to use it, pay for it, and keep coming back?

You don't need to invent a new technology. Sometimes the opportunity is recognizing what existing technology makes possible.

Here are five examples from companies we've invested in at [PWV (Preston-Werner Ventures)](https://www.pwv.com).

## Doctorin' the Product / Last Train to Model Central

<Image
  src={cursorArt}
  alt="Cursor, Doctorin' the Product, after The Timelords' Doctorin' the Tardis"
  width={448}
  height={448}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

### <img src="/logos/cursor.png" alt="Cursor logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Cursor: change the product

We first looked at Cursor at its seed round.

Frankly, the product wasn't great.

We liked the founders. We believed AI would fundamentally change how software gets written. But the product wasn't there yet.

Ten months later, at the Series A, we saw it again.

It was dramatically better.

Same founders. Same thesis. Very different product.

Anysphere had started in 2022 trying an AI email client and an AI-powered CAD tool for mechanical engineers. Their CAD project was even called Cursor at first. They pivoted to code, but they'd already spent considerable time figuring out what to build.

And even after finding the right direction, they kept improving it.

**The lesson: your first idea is a hypothesis, not your destiny.**

### <img src="/logos/fal.png" alt="fal logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> fal: change the market

Where Cursor changed the product, fal changed the market.

Burkay and Gorkem started by building Python data infrastructure for data pipelines, analytics, and ML training. It was highly optimized, but it was not the business that made them successful.

We had been looking for a platform to generate images, video, and music. Most demos were slow or clumsy. Then we saw fal's "Make it Real" collaboration with our portfolio company tldraw: you sketched on screen, and an image appeared milliseconds later.

That was the tell. Their infrastructure was exceptionally good at running generative models at scale. When Stable Diffusion took off, they pointed it at the new market.

Jensen Huang says every pixel will be "generated, not rendered." fal is one of the teams making that true.

Neither started with exactly the business that made it successful. Neither had to invent all the underlying technology. They recognized an opportunity, made something useful out of it, and followed the evidence.

**Sometimes the best opportunity isn't the one you set out to find.**

## What Time Is Fit?

<Image
  src={tursoArt}
  alt="Turso, What Time Is Fit?, after The KLF's What Time Is Love? (Pure Trance 1)"
  width={448}
  height={448}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

### <img src="/logos/turso.png" alt="Turso logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Turso: the market catches up

I consulted with Supabase back in 2021.

Supabase won the previous era by being an all-in-one platform: Postgres, auth, storage, edge functions, and a heavy Docker setup. Training data and vibe-coding tools like Lovable defaulted to it.

Turso built for unlimited databases: one per user, one per agent, one per task. Its multi-tenant isolation and local-first experience were already suited for the agentic era.

Turso was not Supabase yet, and the reason was capital, not product or architecture.

We had already invested at seed. When Glauber was raising a Series A, he believed the future was millions of small databases. At the time, that was an interesting idea, not yet a market.

Then AI agents arrived. They need to spin up a database, use it, and throw it away. Turso had the right shape for that. The team stopped using the word "ephemeral" because it implied the data was unimportant.

In October 2026, Supabase acquired Turso. The company that won the last era bought the company built for the next one.

**Sometimes you don't find the market. The market finds you.**

### <img src="/logos/workbrew.png" alt="Workbrew logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Workbrew: find the buyer

Developers love Homebrew, the free tool for installing software on macOS and Linux. Corporate IT departments do not love employees installing whatever they want. They need visibility, security, and control.

Workbrew built a business in that gap. Developers use the software. Companies pay to manage it.

**The person who uses your product isn't always the person who pays for it.** This is really about understanding people.

Two groups can look at exactly the same product and see completely different problems. Recognizing those differences is often where the business opportunity lies.

## Justified & Validated?

<Image
  src={zooArt}
  alt="Zoo, Justified & Validated, after The KLF's Justified & Ancient"
  width={448}
  height={448}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

### <img src="/logos/zoo.png" alt="Zoo logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Zoo: become the layer

Zoo is another PWV investment, and it shows a different way to take on an entrenched market.

CAD has been one of the most entrenched software markets in the world for over 50 years. The incumbents built deep moats around proprietary file formats, workstation-bound execution, and workflows that were never designed for cloud compute or machine learning. Hardware design data mostly sits in static files that are hard to use as training data.

Zoo rebuilt the CAD stack from first principles. Its code-native design language, KCL, turns design intent into a machine-readable representation. Its geometry engine runs on GPUs and through an API. The team is credible: Jordan Noone co-founded Relativity Space, Jessie Frazelle co-founded Oxide Computer, and Jenna Bryant built early teams at Oculus, Tinder, and Snap.

But Zoo does not need to replace the incumbents to win. It is designed to be adopted incrementally inside existing engineering stacks, and its API-first architecture connects design, analysis, manufacturing, and downstream systems that were previously siloed.

The early proof is Blue Origin, which is feeding millions of existing CAD files into a fine-tuned Text-to-CAD model.

Because it lowers the skill and time required to design, Zoo can expand the market rather than only compete within it. That is how it becomes the AI-native infrastructure layer for hardware engineering.

The analogy to Cursor is direct: rebuild the workflow around the model rather than bolt AI onto the old stack.

**The lesson: you do not have to replace the incumbent to win the market. Become the layer it cannot build itself.**

There's no single formula for product-market fit. Much of what makes great founders successful has little to do with being the best programmer in the room: curiosity, creativity, taste, critical thinking, understanding people, and the willingness to change your mind. Those aren't exclusive to liberal arts graduates, but they're exactly what a liberal arts education develops.

You don't need anyone's permission to recognize a problem worth solving, and you don't have to invent the underlying technology. You can learn the tools, find collaborators, or bring together complementary skills. What matters is having a point of view about how the world could be different, and being willing to find out whether you're right.

Which brings me back to The KLF. They didn't invent the instruments. They took what already existed, combined it in original ways, and made a mark on popular culture. They found an audience, became enormously successful, and even wrote the manual.

Then, in 1992, they quit the music business. Two years later, as the K Foundation, they took a million pounds in cash to a Scottish island called Jura and burned it. Literally.

As a venture capitalist, I've seen plenty of startups burn through a million pounds, but rarely quite so efficiently. They framed it as a deliberate artistic act, not a business failure. And it makes one last point: **finding product-market fit and building a lasting business are two different things.** You can find fit, lose it, or walk away from it.

The KLF had the creativity, taste, and point of view to make something people wanted. That's the connection I want to leave you with: **you don't have to invent the instruments to change the music.** You need an idea, the imagination to do something with it, and enough curiosity to discover whether anyone else cares.

Because ultimately, **you don't get to decide whether the world wants what you've built. The world decides.**
