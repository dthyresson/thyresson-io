> "If you want to do something, go and do it! Don't wait to be asked..." — Bill Drummond, The KLF, [_The Manual_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Manual) (1988)

In 1988, Bill Drummond and Jimmy Cauty released _The Manual (How to Have a Number One the Easy Way)_. They had just taken a knowingly ridiculous mashup, _Doctorin' the Tardis_, to number one in the UK. Then they wrote a book explaining how anyone could do the same.

The pitch was outrageous: you didn't need to be a great musician, invent a new sound, or wait for someone to discover you. Listen to what people respond to. Understand the patterns. Combine familiar ingredients into something new. Get it heard.

It was part satire, part genuine instruction manual. The joke wasn't that pop music was worthless. They loved its power. The joke was the idea that something as elusive as a hit could be reduced to a guaranteed recipe — even though they'd just followed one themselves.

Silicon Valley has been trying to write its own version of _The Manual_ for decades. We call it **product-market fit. PMF.** It's often what separates a promising seed-stage experiment from a business ready for Series A. Does the product work? Does anyone want it? Do they want it enough to use it, pay for it, and come back?

Making a hit record and finding product-market fit have more in common than you might think. Both require understanding what people want, assembling something that resonates, and figuring out how to get it into their hands. The ingredients are easier to describe than the magic that makes them come together. Founders want a formula. Investors want to recognize the hit before everyone else does.

But there's no guarantee.

And the skill isn't always inventing something from scratch.

**It's having taste, a point of view, and the curiosity to discover where an idea really belongs. You don't have to invent the instruments to change the music.**

Here are five examples from companies we've invested in at [PWV (Preston-Werner Ventures)](https://www.pwv.com). Five ways to make — or find — a hit.

## Doctorin' the Product / Last Train to Model Central

### <img src="/logos/cursor.png" alt="Cursor logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Cursor: change the product

We first looked at Cursor at its seed round. Frankly, the product and developer experience — the DX — still needed work. We liked the founders, and we believed AI would fundamentally change how software gets written. But the product wasn't there yet, and a giant incumbent was already moving on the same opportunity.

GitHub Copilot was arriving. GitHub had access to more of the world's code than anyone. We thought it might own the coming explosion in what we now call vibe coding. It didn't. Cursor grabbed the moment instead.

A few months later, at the Series A, we saw Cursor again. Dramatically better. Same founders. Same thesis. A much better product.

Before founding Anysphere, the four founders had spent about six months building an AI tool for mechanical engineers working in CAD. They didn't know that domain, and the idea went nowhere. They changed direction, shipped Cursor in 2023, and kept improving it.

That's a lesson Drummond and Cauty would have recognized. Don't become so attached to your original composition that you stop listening to the audience. The point isn't to defend your first idea. The point is to make something people want.

**The lesson: your first idea is a hypothesis, not your destiny.**

<blockquote className="callout">
  <p>
    <strong>Question:</strong> what's the hypothesis you're currently treating
    as destiny — and what would it take to test it this month?
  </p>
</blockquote>

### <img src="/logos/fal.png" alt="fal logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> fal: change the market

Where Cursor changed the product, fal changed the market.

Burkay and Gorkem started with Python infrastructure for data pipelines, analytics, and ML training. It was fast, carefully engineered technology. But it wasn't yet the business that would make them successful.

We'd been looking for a platform to generate images, video, and music. Most demos were painfully slow or clumsy. Then we saw fal's "Make it Real" collaboration with our portfolio company tldraw: sketch something on a screen, and an image appeared almost immediately.

That was the tell. Their infrastructure was exceptionally good at running generative models at scale. When Stable Diffusion took off, they aimed that capability at a new market.

When we first met the founders, they told us the future was one where every pixel would be "generated, not rendered." They saw a world where applications would need fast, inexpensive inference for generated media — and built for it.

The KLF didn't invent sampling or the sounds they borrowed. They recognized what existing ingredients could become in a new context. fal didn't invent generative AI. Its insight was where the infrastructure it already had could create new value.

**The lesson: sometimes the best opportunity isn't the one you set out to find.**

<blockquote className="callout">
  <p>
    <strong>Question:</strong> what infrastructure or skill you've already built
    could you point at a completely different market?
  </p>
</blockquote>

## What Time Is Fit?

<Image
  src={tursoArt}
  alt="Turso, What Time Is Fit?, after The KLF's What Time Is Love? (Pure Trance 1)"
  width={448}
  height={448}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

### <img src="/logos/turso.png" alt="Turso logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Turso: the market catches up

I consulted with Supabase in 2021 and saw firsthand how it won the previous era by offering an all-in-one platform: Postgres, auth, storage, edge functions, and a substantial Docker setup. Tools such as Lovable later made that kind of backend the default for many AI-built applications.

Turso made a different bet: countless inexpensive SQLite databases, potentially one per user, agent, or task. Its multi-tenant isolation and local-first experience were well suited to a world of autonomous software.

PWV had already invested in Turso's angel and seed rounds. Glauber believed the future would involve millions of small databases. At the time, it was a point of view, not a fully formed market.

Then AI agents arrived. Agents need to create state as easily as creating a file, without provisioning a dedicated server for every tiny workload. Databases on demand, cheap enough to be disposable, but durable when they need to be. Turso had the right shape.

In October 2026, the company that helped define the previous era moved to acquire the company built for the next one.

_The Manual_ could teach you the structure of a hit song. It couldn't tell you what people would want to hear two years later. That takes conviction and timing. Being early can look indistinguishable from being wrong — until the audience arrives.

**The lesson: sometimes you don't find the market. The market finds you.**

<blockquote className="callout">
  <p>
    <strong>Question:</strong> what's a bet you're making on where the market
    will be in two years — and how will you know if you're early or just wrong?
  </p>
</blockquote>

### <img src="/logos/workbrew.png" alt="Workbrew logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Workbrew: find the buyer

Developers love Homebrew, the free tool for installing software on macOS and Linux. Corporate IT departments don't necessarily love employees installing whatever they want. They need visibility, security, and control.

Workbrew built a business in that gap. Developers use the software. Companies pay to manage it.

The person who uses your product isn't always the person who pays for it. And those two people may want opposite things: freedom on one side, governance on the other. A useful product has to understand both.

One of the more sincere parts of _The Manual_ is how much attention it gives to everything beyond making the record: production, promotion, distribution, and getting it into shops. A good song nobody hears isn't a hit. Likewise, good software that never reaches its buyer isn't much of a business.

**The lesson: know who's using it, who's paying for it, and what each of them actually wants.**

<blockquote className="callout">
  <p>
    <strong>Question:</strong> what's a free tool developers use that companies
    would pay to bring under control — and what would each side say they're
    buying?
  </p>
</blockquote>

## Justified & Validated?

<Image
  src={zooArt}
  alt="Zoo, Justified & Validated, after The KLF's Justified & Ancient"
  width={448}
  height={448}
  class="shadow-lg mx-auto"
/>

### <img src="/logos/zoo.png" alt="Zoo logo" width="24" height="24" class="inline-block h-6 w-6 rounded align-middle mr-2 my-0" /> Zoo: reinvent the instrument

Zoo, another PWV investment, offers a different way to take on an entrenched market: reinvent it for the AI era.

CAD has been one of software's most stubborn categories for over 50 years: proprietary file formats, workstation-bound execution, and workflows never designed for cloud computing or AI. Valuable design data sits in static files, difficult for machines to understand or use as training data.

Zoo rebuilt the CAD stack from first principles. KCL turns design intent into code, a GPU-backed geometry engine runs through an API, and both integrate with existing engineering workflows. Blue Origin is already feeding millions of CAD files into a fine-tuned Text-to-CAD model — early evidence of how much more the market could do.

There's a parallel to Cursor: rather than bolt AI onto legacy tools, Zoo is rebuilding the workflow around AI.

The analogy with The KLF isn't that Zoo merely samples someone else's work. Zoo is doing difficult foundational engineering. It's that a new creative language or interface can open an existing medium to entirely new participants and possibilities. Sometimes the opportunity isn't to replace the instruments. It's to change how people can play them.

**The lesson: you don't have to displace the incumbents to reinvent an industry. Don't just improve the existing tools. Change what people can do with them.**

<blockquote className="callout">
  <p>
    <strong>Question:</strong> what fifty-year-old industry would you rebuild
    for AI?
  </p>
</blockquote>

There's no single formula for product-market fit. If there were, every startup could follow the steps, just as everyone who read _The Manual_ would have a number-one hit.

Look at what these stories require. Cursor had to change its product. fal saw a new use for the capabilities it had built. Turso committed to a future that wasn't yet obvious. Workbrew understood that the user and the buyer were different people. Zoo is reinventing the tools with which an entire industry works.

Some of that takes exceptional engineering. But the common thread isn't simply technical skill. It's curiosity, imagination, taste, critical thinking, an understanding of people, and a willingness to change your mind. Those skills aren't exclusive to liberal arts students, but they're among the advantages of a liberal arts education: the ability to cross disciplines, question assumptions, and recognize connections others miss.

You don't need permission to start. You don't necessarily need to invent the underlying technology. You can learn the tools, find collaborators, and assemble existing ideas in surprising ways. But you **do** need a point of view. And then you need to find out whether anyone else shares it.

Which brings me back to The KLF.

They made hit records out of familiar ingredients, wrote a manual explaining how to do it, and became enormously successful. Then, in 1992, they quit the music business. Two years later, as the K Foundation, they took a million pounds in cash to the Scottish island of Jura and burned it. Literally.

As a venture capitalist, I've seen plenty of startups burn through a million pounds, but rarely quite so efficiently.

The act was deliberately provocative. It also complicates the promise of their own book. They'd achieved the hits, fame, and money. And then they walked away. A number-one record isn't necessarily a lasting career. Product-market fit isn't necessarily a lasting company. And even success doesn't answer the question of what was worth making in the first place.

<blockquote className="callout">
  <p>
    <strong>Fit isn't a finish line.</strong> You find it, lose it, and re-earn
    it. The Manual might tell you how to make a hit. It can't tell you what is
    worth making.
  </p>
</blockquote>

Here's what I'd leave you with. The KLF didn't need to invent the instruments to change the music. They needed creativity, taste, conviction, and enough understanding of an audience to make something people wanted to hear.

**You don't have to invent the instruments to change the music. But you do have to make something people want to hear.**

And you don't have to wait for anyone to give you permission to try.
